§1 · India · Loss and damage · 2026

Relief arrives. Recovery does not.

India pays for climate disasters through relief rules written for emergencies. For what comes after, and for losses that arrive slowly, there is money on paper and little of it released. So who answers for the rest?

Movement I · The system

One loss, four stages

"Loss and damage" is the United Nations term for the harm climate change causes that people could not prevent or adapt to. India has no law that uses the term. A disaster management system does part of the job.

  • §2The journey of a loss

§2 · How it works

The journey of a loss

TakeawayIndia reports loss and damage to the UN "under the rubric of disaster management". There is no separate law, fund or body.

Every loss passes through the same four stages. A cyclone, a heatwave or a river eating a village is first forecast or not, then counted or not, then paid for or not, and then the people affected either recover or do not. The rest of this page follows that order.

Avert and warn

Forecasts, alerts and works that reduce harm before it lands.

Radars, flood forecasts, mitigation funds
Record and assess

Counting what was lost: lives, homes, crops, land.

State reports, police records, weather agency counts
Pay and relieve

Fixed payments for a fixed list of calamities.

State and national relief funds, crop insurance
Recover and relocate

Rebuilding, and moving people who cannot go back.

Recovery window, erosion resettlement policy

Source for the quotation: India's first Biennial Transparency Report to the UNFCCC.

§4 · Counting the dead

One year, three death counts

TakeawayFor 2022, official counts of deaths from extreme weather run from 2,227 to 8,060.

Police records and the weather agency count the same year very differently. The weather agency itself published two totals. For heat, the gap is widest: 730 deaths in police records against 30 in the weather agency's count.

The national table that does not add up

The Home Ministry compiles losses reported by states. For 2025-26 (provisional) it prints 6,631 lives lost, but the state rows sum to 6,683. It prints 2,02,364 houses damaged, but the state rows sum to 1,56,364. No correction was found. In the same reply the Ministry says it "does not centrally maintain data regarding the loss of lives and property due to any disaster in the country". Source: Lok Sabha Unstarred Question 2757, 4 August 2026.

Why the counts differ

The series use different definitions, different reporting chains and different years. Police records cover calendar years and all "forces of nature". The Home Ministry table covers financial years and what states report. Financial year and calendar year figures are not comparable, so this page never adds them together.

§5 · Beyond the sudden disaster

The slow and the wide

TakeawayA third of the coastline is eroding. Erosion is not on the list of calamities that qualify for relief.

Some losses arrive over years, or are spread thinly across millions of people. They are measured, but the relief system was not built around them.

Movement III · The bill

Who pays, and how much

Relief has fixed rates and large funds behind it. What states ask for and what they receive are far apart, and the money for what comes after has barely moved.

§6 · The relief rates

What relief pays

TakeawayRelief rates rose from 2026-27, and death relief doubled. Heatwave and lightning now qualify.

Fourteen calamities qualify for the state and national relief funds. For each, the Home Ministry fixes what can be paid. These are the main rates, before and after the October 2026 revision.

What the 2025 change to the law did

The Disaster Management (Amendment) Act 2025, in force from 9 April 2025, removed three sections of the 2005 Act. The duty to set minimum standards of relief became a function of the national authority to "recommend guidelines" (Section 6(2)(w)). The rule that states may not go below those standards was kept (Section 18(2)(o)). The section on relief in repayment of loans was removed and nothing replaces it. The words "compensation and" were removed from Section 61. Source: Gazette text of Act 10 of 2025.

§7 · The gap

Asked for, and released

TakeawayStates received under 18% of what they asked the national disaster fund for between 2020 and 2024.

When a disaster is too large for a state's own fund, the state sends a memorandum to the Centre. A World Bank study for the 16th Finance Commission added up 19 states' requests and the releases against them.

Source: World Bank, Closing the Financing Gap in Disaster Risk Management, Annex 1. State requests are themselves contested: the national auditor recorded a central team's finding that one state's assessment was "exaggerated and unrealistically high" (see the Sundarbans case in §9).

      Relief has rules. What comes after does not yet.
